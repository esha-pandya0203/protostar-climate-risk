# Policy Idea: Plan cooling for the climate we forecast, not the one we had

**What risk did we notice?** Utilities, grid planners and cities size cooling capacity using 30-year "normals", and in a warming climate those normals fall behind. In our backtests, which forecast seven years ahead from start years 2000–2011 using only earlier data, the 30-year average underestimated cooling degree days (CDD) by 118 °F-days per city-year. Actual CDD exceeded the normal in 70% of city-years. Our 2019–2025 forecast is above the 1989–2018 normal in all 207 scored cities, by a median of 11%. The relative gap is largest in northern cities that have historically needed little cooling: Minneapolis +20%, Chicago +16% and Pittsburgh +13%, while Seattle is +41% from a low base.

**What would we do about it, and who would do it?** State public utility commissions should require utilities to use forward-looking cooling-demand projections alongside 30-year normals in their resource plans and peak-load forecasts, and to update them every year. A trend-adjusted forecast like ours is one example. In the same backtest, our ensemble halved the cold bias (−118 to −58 °F-days) and cut the error by 12% compared with the normal. Cities should use the same projections to decide where to put cooling help first: where the gap between the old normal and the forecast is largest, such as schools, older housing and cooling centres in northern cities.

**Why is it sustainable?** Seeing demand coming early leaves time to meet it with the cheapest and cleanest options, such as efficiency upgrades, heat pumps, cool roofs, shade trees and demand-response programs. Otherwise the shortfall shows up in a heat wave and is met with emergency power purchases or new fossil-fuel peaker plants. The method uses public data and simple, transparent models, so any utility or city can rerun it each year at almost no cost.
